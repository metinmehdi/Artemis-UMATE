UMATE

Practise the metro in VR. Then ride it for real.

UMATE helps young people with autism, intellectual disability and other cognitive differences learn to travel on the Baku Metro and pay with BakıKart on their own. The learner puts an ordinary smartphone into a low-cost VR box and rehearses their own trip: top up the card, pass the turnstile, choose the right platform, get off at the right station. A calm AI voice buddy is beside them. A trainer watches every step live on a laptop. When the rehearsal is over, the same phone leaves the box and becomes the learner's guide on the real trip. On stage, one judge wears the headset while a second judge picks a problem from the laptop, for example "not enough balance". The first judge has to solve it in VR, and the audience sees both views.

WhoYoung people with cognitive differences, their trainers (rehabilitation centres, inclusive schools) and families.
WhatA phone and VR-box rehearsal of the Baku Metro and BakıKart payments, built from real station photos.
HowLook-to-select, a voice AI buddy, step-by-step support that fades over time, and a live trainer panel.
Why it mattersWithout travel training, people depend on others or on costly special transport. Practising safely costs less and builds confidence before the real trip.

1The problem

Many young people with autism or intellectual disability can learn to travel alone. What they lack is safe, repeated practice, and digital payment makes the first trip even harder.

Why the Baku Metro is a digital barrier

  • Entry is only through turnstiles that read a BakıKart (plastic or paper), a QR ticket from the BakıKart app, or an NFC bank card. Validation happens at entry.
  • Cards are topped up at kiosks with several screens and languages (Azerbaijani, Russian, English). A wrong choice, a card that is not read, or "not enough balance" in a crowd is exactly the moment many learners freeze.
  • Choosing the platform means reading a sign "towards" a terminus station, not towards your destination. This is abstract and hard for many learners.

What happens today

Travel training is done one-to-one in the real metro: a trainer goes with the learner, often for many trips. It works, but it is expensive, stressful in crowds, hard to repeat and hard to measure. Existing VR tools need standalone headsets and free floor space (details in section 4).

2The solution and how it works

UMATE turns any smartphone and a cheap VR box into a safe rehearsal of the learner's own metro trip. An AI buddy stays beside the learner and a trainer watches live.

The trip: 4 steps, each with a problem the learner practises

#StepWhat the learner doesProblem practised
1Top up at the BakıKart kioskLooks at the kiosk, places the card, chooses an amount, checks the new balance.Card not read
2Pass the turnstileTaps the card on the reader and waits for the green signal.Not enough balance, so go back and top up
3Choose the platformReads the two direction signs and picks "towards İçərişəhər" to reach 28 May.Delay or platform change announced
4Ride and get off PlanListens to announcements, counts stops, gets off at the right station.Crowd and noise

How the learner interacts, without a controller

  • Look around: the phone's gyroscope turns the view 360°. The learner sits on a swivel chair.
  • Choose: looking at an object for 1.5 seconds selects it, while a ring fills up. A tap on the screen, the VR box button or a Bluetooth remote also works.
  • Walk: looking at a target on the floor moves the learner there with a short fade. There is no smooth movement, which is the main cause of motion sickness.
  • Talk to the buddy: looking at the glowing orb opens the microphone. In a noisy room it does not listen otherwise.

Support that fades, and mistakes that are allowed

  • If the learner is stuck, help comes in steps: first the buddy gives a short spoken hint, then an arrow appears, then the right object is highlighted.
  • Mistakes are allowed. The buddy explains calmly and the learner tries again. The panel records each mistake.
  • Asking for help counts as success, as it does in special education practice.

The trainer panel and the stage demo

  • The laptop shows a live view of what the learner sees (no video is streamed; the laptop renders the same scene from the learner's head position), plus the step timeline, the level of help used, the AI conversation as text and the virtual card balance.
  • The trainer, or a second judge, can trigger a problem: not enough balance, card not read, train delay or platform change, crowd and noise.
  • After the rehearsal, the phone leaves the box and switches to guide mode: the same steps as simple picture and voice cards, plus an "I need help" button.

Honest boundary. Success in VR is evidence of practice, not a certificate that the learner is ready to travel alone. The trainer decides when to try the real trip.

4Research and competitors

VR travel training looks promising, but its transfer to real life is the weakest part of the evidence. We therefore design UMATE around the bridge to the real trip.

What the research says

StudyFindingLimitation
Simões et al., 2018, JMIR Serious GamesA VR bus-travel game for autistic participants (n=10, 1–3 sessions). Knowledge of bus riding rose significantly, in-game success was high (93.8% reported), and electrodermal activity, a marker of anxiety, fell inside the virtual bus.Small proof of concept with no autistic control group.

Data each session creates

A time-stamped event log stored on the trainer's laptop. It records:

  • session start;
  • step reached;
  • what the learner looked at and chose;
  • mistakes;
  • help shown (level and reason);
  • problems triggered and solved;
  • card transactions (balance before and after, success or failure);
  • the AI conversation as text only;
  • head direction 5 times a second;
  • a summary of each step (time, mistakes, highest help level, whether help was asked for).

All numbers on the panel are calculated from this log.

Real versus sample data

The live session on stage is real. Charts showing progress over several weeks use a synthetic group of learners, clearly labelled "SAMPLE", until pilot data exists. We never present sample data as evidence that UMATE works.

Privacy

  • No login: learners appear as nicknames.
  • Data stays on the trainer's laptop. No audio is recorded or stored.
  • During a session, the learner's voice is processed by the AI provider to produce the answer.
  • A real product needs guardian consent, a data-processing agreement, and an option to turn the voice off.

Our own AI, on the roadmap

  • Readiness prediction model Plan:
    • In the pilot, trainers record whether the real trip succeeded.
    • A model learns which VR behaviours predict real independence.
    • This turns our weakest point (VR is not real life) into a measurable advantage, using data nobody else collects.
    • It creates a loop: more sessions give better predictions, which earn more trust, which bring more centres and more sessions.
  • Later: Azerbaijani speech recognition for atypical speech, and a small AI that runs on the phone itself for privacy and offline use.

We do not claim to have trained our own model yet. Today we use existing AI services carefully, and we design the data collection that a future model needs.

8Hard questions and answers

These are the questions we expect from judges, with answers that do not overclaim.

1. VR is not real life. Will the skills transfer?

We agree that transfer is the weakest point in the research. That is why we use the learner's own real route, use real station photos, keep the same steps in guide mode on the real trip, and let trainers record real-trip outcomes. VR success is never treated as a readiness certificate.

2. Why not just use Changefied or Floreo?

They prove there is demand. Changefied needs a standalone headset and a 2×2 m space, and Floreo offers general lessons. UMATE runs on any phone with a VR box, trains BakıKart payment and its failure cases on the local route, connects to the real trip through guide mode, and lets a trainer trigger problems live.

3. What about motion sickness and sensory overload?

There is no smooth movement (only teleport), the learner sits, and first sessions last 2–3 minutes. Visuals are calm, the trainer controls the noise level, and the buddy can lead a breathing exercise. This follows the gradual-acclimation advice of Schmidt et al. (2021).

4. Isn't sending minors' voices to OpenAI a privacy problem?

On stage, adults use the demo. The microphone opens only when the learner looks at the buddy. No audio is stored, only text on the trainer's laptop. A real product would require guardian consent, offer a mode without voice (pre-recorded lines) and, later, AI running on the phone.

5. Why is the interface in English if the users are Azerbaijani?

English was chosen for the camp pitch. All interface text is in a single language file, so an Azerbaijani pack is the next step. Station names already appear in Azerbaijani, as on real signs. The quality of the AI voice in Azerbaijani has to be tested first.

6. What if there is no internet?

VR, the panel and the logs run on the laptop's local network. Only the AI voice needs the internet, and without it the buddy switches to pre-recorded lines.

7. How will you prove it works?

Today we measure behaviour in VR: time, mistakes, help level and help requests. In the pilot we add knowledge checks before and after, and trainer-recorded outcomes of real trips. We do not claim proven effectiveness yet.

8. Is the dashboard data real?

The live stage session is real. Multi-week trend charts use synthetic data that is clearly labelled "SAMPLE" until we have pilot data.

9. Can the AI give wrong directions?

The buddy receives the facts from the scenario engine and is told never to invent stations or prices. Whether an answer is right is decided by the engine, not by the AI. The trainer sees every transcript.

10. Who pays, and why would they?

Centres and schools pay, because UMATE saves trainer time and provides progress reports. Families use guide mode for free. Prices will be validated in the pilot.

11. Is a cheap VR box good enough?

For seated rehearsal, head rotation is enough. We designed for it: look-to-select and teleport. UMATE also works without a box by moving the phone in the hand. Testing on Android and iPhone is under way.

12. Did you really build this yourselves?

The code is public on GitHub with its full history, and every change was approved by three teammates. We openly used AI coding assistants. The architecture, the product decisions and the testing are ours.

13. How do you scale to other cities?

Routes come from open transit data (6,000+ GTFS feeds worldwide) and OpenStreetMap, the rules from a file, and the scenes from local photos. Language is a text file. None of this requires new code.

14. What if a learner panics in VR?

The learner can look away or take the phone out at any time. The trainer has a "calm down" button, the buddy guides a slow breath, and the session can stop at once. The trainer stays next to the learner.
